When the page still works, whose policy does it carry?

When clinical-trial sponsors move data requests to a new portal, their older pages can remain in circulation. In three historical ClinicalStudyDataRequest (CSDR) pages, the transition notice identified the expected sponsor, but the policy body matched a different company. A reader could reach a working page and still encounter the wrong sponsor's access conditions.

The study grew out of a route audit following 14 trial records across five sponsors. Tracking where older listings and request instructions led brought a second question into view: whether the policy attached to a sponsor page came from that sponsor. Archived source comparisons became the central evidence for the paper.

Three historical policy-body mismatches

Fifteen date-stamped Common Crawl WARC responses anchor the reproducible comparisons. Each record has a documented archive location and cryptographic hash, allowing the original page text to be checked again.

Why attribution matters

A transition notice may point to the right destination while a policy underneath it describes someone else's eligibility rules, release timing, or data scope. Those details matter to researchers planning a request. The cases make a practical case for checking the relationship between the named sponsor, the policy text, and the request route—not just whether a link opens.

A record others can verify

The reproduction package contains the public WARC coordinates, hashes, comparison scripts, tests, and expected outputs. Its frozen Zenodo v1.0.0 archive supplies a version-specific citation. Original third-party web pages are retrieved from Common Crawl when the checks run.

The earlier trial-route ledger and exploratory page comparisons remain available as context. The 15 preserved WARC coordinates support the exact historical replays. The work documents policy attribution on selected pages, rather than a platform-wide failure rate or the outcome of any individual data request.
