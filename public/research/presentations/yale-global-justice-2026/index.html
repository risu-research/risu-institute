Global Access, Unequal Control: The Locality Tax in Managed AI

Who can choose where AI processes information?

In January 2024, a question in Singapore's Parliament asked about the private firms and foreign jurisdictions involved when government agencies use AI. The question points to a practical purchasing problem. A supplier may offer local data storage while processing prompts and responses elsewhere.

This research compares the processing choices that four major managed AI platforms published for government and institutional buyers. The relevant choice depends on the country, the exact model, and how the service is purchased. A location named on a supplier website may cover storage, inference processing, or a larger multi-country zone.

A narrow map of documented choices

The original survey checked published options across 195 countries using a fixed supplier catalogue dated 5 October 2026. Under a strict national-processing criterion, 19 countries had at least one qualifying option. The count increased to 42 when the rule also admitted documented processing zones that span multiple countries.

In the same fixed survey, none of the 25 low-income countries met the strict rule, compared with 16 of 64 high-income countries. The 19 qualifying countries accounted for about 35% of the world's population. That population share reflects where residents live; it does not measure actual access to an eligible service.

The buying plan can change the answer

Later supplier documents made the purchasing issue concrete. A model could list national processing under reserved capacity while its ordinary pay-as-you-go menu listed broader regions. Other options depended on customer approval, model eligibility or a price premium. The location promise had to be read alongside the purchase route.

A follow-up audit coded 812 public catalogue entries as of 31 October 2026. With pay-as-you-go purchasing alone, the audit identified 29 documented country–platform pairs across 19 countries. Under a broader profile that admitted approvals and reserved capacity, the same catalogue yielded 45 pairs across 22 countries. The difference comes from changing the purchasing conditions applied to the same records, not from a change in market availability over time.

A stricter price cross-check linked an exact model, processing location, purchasing method and numeric official price for 34 routes. Those routes supported an illustrative budget calculation. The other entries remained outside that stricter price-linked subset, and the audit did not test completed purchases, live traffic routing or customer-specific entitlements.

What the locality tax captures

The locality tax describes the extra cost or conditions attached to choosing where managed AI processes information. It may appear as a smaller choice of models, a narrower processing promise, an additional approval, a capacity reservation or a higher price. These conditions differ across suppliers and models. They also change as services and contract terms change.

A dated record for every locality claim

The presentation proposes a Locality Disclosure Matrix that would bring seven facts into one record: data storage, inference processing, model and version, purchase route, price and capacity, guarantees and exclusions, and the dates on which those commitments apply. Each entry would point to a dated source and identify who is responsible for checking it.

The proposed decision process begins with a procurement screen, continues through the negotiated contract, and reaches the operational setup. A buyer could compare the advertised option with its own requirements, identify the missing evidence, and revisit the choice when the model, region, contract or capacity changes.

A small preliminary pilot applied the seven fields to selected public documents from four suppliers. Sixteen of 28 entries were directly documented, eight required cross-document checks, three were only partly confirmed and one numeric price link remained unresolved. This was a one-analyst document pilot. The matrix and its proposed contract and operational checks remain subjects for further testing.
